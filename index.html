Every cleaning decision was measured, not assumed

Each posting has two possible sources for the same fields: the job API and the AI reading the description. I checked a random sample by hand to see which source filled each field correctly, then built the pipeline to take every field from the source that does it best.

100% vs 53%

Share of postings where work setting and education were filled: 100% from the API, only 53% from the AI, because many descriptions never state them. So these two fields come from the API, and the AI handles skills, tools and industry, which the API does not provide.

1,467 → 28

Free-text industries grouped into 28 categories, covering 98.8% of postings.

203

True duplicates removed by using the posting URL as the key, with no valid rows lost.

2 silent breaks

Made the pipeline resistant to API changes

The pipeline broke twice because of changes on the provider's side, with no error raised. First, the API retired its endpoint and runs reported success for eight days while collecting nothing. Then I found its job IDs changed on every call, so one posting had been stored eleven times. I made the posting URL the primary key and added a daily record count, so a provider update can no longer break the data silently.

Known limit: the same job posted on two different platforms has two URLs, so it is still counted twice. That cannot be controlled from my side.

6 sources → 1

Verified the data against an outside benchmark

The first version used six free job APIs and collected about 100,000 postings within days, more than national labor statistics suggest exist. The AI assistant helping me reported no issue. I inspected the raw table, found heavy duplication, and replaced the six sources with one reliable paid API. Data quality improved immediately.

Case study · Investment analysis

Where does an Airbnb actually pay off?

If you bought a 1–2 bedroom apartment to rent on Airbnb, which city would give the best return? I cleaned Inside Airbnb listings for 123 cities, matched them with property prices and utility costs from Numbeo, converted 35 currencies with exchange-rate data, estimated occupancy, and ranked 80 cities by annual return after running costs.

A screening exercise, not investment advice. The results rest on several assumptions and data limitations: figures are before tax, local regulation is not modelled, occupancy is estimated from reviews, and every city is costed on the same 60 m² apartment. All are documented in the full analysis ↗.

All 80 cities, by return on a city-centre purchase

Every top-20 city is in North America or Western Europe. That is partly a real result and partly the data: coverage is thin in Africa and Asia, and the Middle East is not covered.

Top 10 cities by annual return

Where you buy inside a city can matter as much as which city. In Columbus, the return goes from 9.7% in the centre to 19.8% outside it.

Occupancy predicts return best. Its correlation with return is 0.81 on a scale from 0 to 1, against 0.36 for nightly price.

lose money wherever you buy in them: Oslo, Hong Kong and Munich. Eight lose money on a city-centre purchase.

60 m²

Every city was costed on the same apartment size, since no source covers 80 cities. I re-ran it at 85 m²: cities moved 1.2 places on average, and the top 20 by one place at most.

The top 20: 17 in North America, 3 in Europe

12 US cities, 5 Canadian, plus Edinburgh, Barcelona and Valencia. Chicago leads on a city-centre purchase at 11.5%. Outside the centre, New Orleans leads at 22.5%.

Why the size assumption favours North America

Every city was costed as a 60 m² apartment. Real apartments in the US and Canada are larger (about 68–102 m² in the US), so their purchase cost is understated and their return overstated. Dense cities like Paris or Hong Kong, with smaller apartments, get the opposite effect.

Limited coverage

The data only covers cities that Inside Airbnb's volunteers publish. Africa has two cities, and South Asia and the Middle East have none. This is not a ranking of every city in the world.

30nights a year

Validation

The occupancy model knew nothing about local laws. For Amsterdam, it landed exactly on the legal limit.

Occupancy was estimated from review counts alone. Amsterdam came out at 30 booked nights a year, which is the city's legal cap on holiday rentals. That is strong evidence the method measures something real.

Nightly prices were also checked against commercial data. Edinburgh: $332 here, against $311 (AirROI) and $283 (AirDNA).
